CEE-PEW — Second technical review
Scope: cryptographic construction, protocol mathematics, nonce management, replay protection, framing, reliability, and implementation consistency.
Having examined the more detailed protocol descriptions, I want to take this review a step further than the previous one.
There is an important distinction I want to maintain throughout: a cryptographic design can be mathematically sound in principle while its particular implementation or protocol integration remains insecure.
I am therefore evaluating three separate things:
What the manuscript claims the protocol does.
Whether the described construction supports those claims.
Whether the experimental evidence demonstrates that the implementation actually behaves that way.
I have already identified one particularly important documentation inconsistency: the manuscript describes the GATT identity exchange as being encrypted using a key derived from the four-character code, but its subsequent key-schedule description derives the Ascon key from X25519 through HKDF. Those are materially different constructions. We need to establish which one the firmware actually implements before making a definitive security assessment.
1. Pairing protocol: the most serious security weakness
Critical
Let's examine the construction mathematically.
Your manuscript defines:
\[ C=SHA256( D\parallel id_A\parallel id_B\parallel code_{32} \parallel t_{round} ) \]
where:
\(D\) is the domain-separation string.
\(id_A,id_B\) are the peer identifiers.
\(code_{32}\) is the four-character code repeated eight times.
\(t_{round}=0\).
The code has:
\[ 36^4=1,679,616 \]
possible combinations.
What an attacker can do
An attacker captures the BLE discovery commitment and knows the other inputs.
They can calculate:
\[ C_i=SHA256(D\parallel id_A\parallel id_B \parallel code_{i,32}\parallel 0) \]
for every possible code.
Then they compare each result with the captured commitment.
This is an entirely offline attack. It does not require communicating with either ESP32.
The attacker can enumerate approximately 1.68 million possibilities, which is a very small search space for modern hardware.
My assessment
Your manuscript correctly identifies this weakness.
However, I would make one additional distinction.
There are actually two different cryptographic operations during pairing:
Operation
Purpose
SHA-256 commitment
Establishes code/transcript consistency
Ascon-protected GATT identity exchange
Protects the exchanged identity material
The manuscript says the GATT exchange is protected using the four-character code, while the subsequent session key schedule uses X25519 and HKDF.
These should be described as separate keys and separate protocol stages.
If the GATT encryption key is directly recoverable from the four-character code, recovering the code through the commitment also compromises that exchange.
Recommended correction
Replace the current commitment-based authentication design with a properly specified PAKE.
Your proposed SPAKE2+ migration is reasonable. RFC 9383 specifies a protocol for establishing shared keys using a password without disclosing the password itself.
RFC Editor+1
One caveat: SPAKE2+ is not simply a drop-in replacement for your hash commitment. Its registration, key derivation, transcript binding, identity handling, and key-confirmation procedures all need to be implemented correctly.
2. HKDF and the key schedule
Major — cryptographic specification
Your manuscript defines:
\[ m=Mix(c) \]
\[ s=SHA256(m\parallel c) \]
\[ PRK=HMAC\text{-}SHA256(s,K) \]
where \(K\) is the X25519 shared secret.
Then:
\[ K_{ascon}=HKDF\text{-}Expand(PRK,info_{ascon},16) \]
\[ K_{box}=HKDF\text{-}Expand(PRK,info_{box},32) \]
\[ K_{hop}=HKDF\text{-}Expand(PRK,info_{hop},L) \]
What is correct?
The overall construction is sensible.
X25519 supplies the shared secret.
HKDF extracts and expands keying material.
Distinct information labels separate the derived keys.
The resulting key lengths are appropriate for the stated algorithms.
The use of domain-separated HKDF labels is a good design decision.
What concerns me?
A. The deterministic mixer does not create entropy.
Your Mix(c) preprocessor may produce a different representation of the pairing code, but it does not increase its entropy.
A deterministic transformation cannot turn a four-character password into a genuinely stronger secret.
I would therefore stop presenting this as an independent security layer.
Describe it as a deterministic salt preprocessor.
B. Your key schedule needs complete transcript binding.
I would like to see the exact information strings and whether they incorporate:
Protocol identifier and version.
Initiator and responder identities.
Both ephemeral public keys.
Session identifier.
Negotiated configuration/profile.
Direction or sender role.
Separate labels are good, but they do not automatically prevent unknown-key-share, cross-session, or role-confusion problems.
C. Explain key erasure.
You claim forward secrecy through ephemeral X25519.
That claim depends on more than generating ephemeral key pairs.
The manuscript should specify when the following are erased:
Ephemeral private X25519 keys.
Raw X25519 shared secret.
HKDF PRK.
Previous session keys.
Temporary GATT bootstrap material.
I would particularly like to see whether the raw shared secret remains in a stack buffer after HKDF expansion.
My verdict
Construction broadly sound
I do not see a fundamental mathematical problem with the described HKDF extract-and-expand structure.
However, the preprocessor should not be credited with additional entropy, and the transcript binding and secret-erasure procedures need more explicit documentation.
3. Nonce construction and uniqueness
Major — requires implementation verification
This is an area where your paper is surprisingly detailed.
You describe a 24-byte nonce:
\[ N=session\_id_8\parallel counter_{8,LE}\parallel 0^8 \]
The two encryption algorithms consume it differently:
Algorithm
Nonce consumed
Ascon-128 v1.2
First 16 bytes
XSalsa20
All 24 bytes
Your counters are partitioned:
Initiator: even values.
Responder: odd values.
Each counter advances by two.
Mathematical assessment
The construction has a reasonable uniqueness strategy.
The two directions cannot accidentally allocate the same counter within the same session, assuming the parity assignment is fixed and enforced.
Furthermore, your independent HKDF-derived keys mean the two encryption algorithms do not share the same key.
There is no immediate mathematical reason that their different nonce lengths create a cross-primitive nonce-reuse problem.
However, there are four things I would require before approving this construction.
1. Session identifier generation
How exactly is session_id8 generated?
I want to know:
Whether it comes from a cryptographically secure random source.
Whether it is regenerated for every new key schedule.
Whether it can survive a device reboot.
Whether two sessions can reuse the same identifier and traffic key.
2. Counter reset behaviour
What happens when:
The sender reboots.
A session is re-established.
The receiver reconnects.
A re-key fails halfway through.
The device receives an old frame from a previous session?
The critical rule is that a nonce must never be reused with the same encryption key.
3. Counter overflow
Your counter limit needs to be explicitly stated.
You should define what happens at the limit: reject further encryption and establish a fresh session rather than allowing wraparound.
4. Directional key separation
I would prefer independent directional encryption keys:
\[ K_{A\rightarrow B}\neq K_{B\rightarrow A} \]
Even with parity-separated counters, directional keys provide a cleaner security boundary.
They also simplify future protocol extensions.
An important distinction
Your parity mechanism is useful, but it is not a replacement for proper session-key lifecycle management.
I would retain it while considering directional key derivation in the next revision.
The Ascon specification also makes nonce uniqueness a fundamental requirement for its security guarantees.

5. Outer XSalsa20-HMAC construction
Major — needs a formal construction definition
This is where I would be particularly demanding as a reviewer.
You have designed a custom outer encryption layer rather than using standard NaCl crypto_box.
Your stated construction is:
Encrypt the inner Ascon ciphertext using XSalsa20.
Authenticate the resulting ciphertext using truncated HMAC-SHA256.
Verify the HMAC before decryption.
Verify the inner Ascon authentication tag.
Verify the Ed25519 signature.
The general Encrypt-then-MAC approach is appropriate.
However, I would like to see the exact equations.
For example, something resembling:
\[ C_{outer}=XSalsa20(K_{box},N_{box},C_{inner}) \]
\[ T_{outer}=Trunc_{128} \left(HMAC_{K_{mac}}(M_{authenticated})\right) \]
Notice that I have deliberately used a separate \(K_{mac}\) here.
Your manuscript describes a 32-byte outer-box key but does not sufficiently establish whether the encryption and MAC keys are independently derived.
Four questions need answers
A. Is the HMAC key independent?
If the same 32-byte key is used directly for XSalsa20 and HMAC-SHA256, I would recommend changing that.
Derive separate keys:
\[ K_{enc}=HKDF(PRK,\text{"outer-enc"}) \]
\[ K_{mac}=HKDF(PRK,\text{"outer-mac"}) \]
This makes the construction easier to analyse and avoids unnecessary cross-protocol key reuse.
B. What exactly does HMAC authenticate?
I would expect the authenticated input to cover at least:
The outer nonce.
The ciphertext.
Any relevant protocol context.
If the nonce is not authenticated, its manipulation should be explicitly analysed.
Your inner Ascon authentication may ultimately reject the resulting corrupted plaintext, but that is not a reason to leave the outer construction underspecified.
C. What does Ed25519 actually sign?
The manuscript says the signature covers the outer ciphertext.
Does it also cover:
The ESL header?
The outer nonce?
The HMAC tag?
The frame length?
You state that the Ascon associated data binds the ESL header, which helps, but I want the precise signed transcript defined.
For example:
\[ S=Ed25519.Sign(sk_{sender},H(context\parallel frame)) \]
This is illustrative, not a claim about your current implementation.
D. What happens if verification fails?
I would like to see whether all failures produce the same externally observable behaviour, including timing and feedback.
You already describe silent discard, which is sensible.
However, identical error responses alone do not establish the absence of timing or side-channel oracles.
My recommendation
Do not market the outer layer primarily as an additional 128-bit security guarantee.
Instead, explain its two intended roles:
An independently keyed cryptographic hedge.
An inexpensive authentication filter before expensive public-key verification.
The second is a much stronger engineering justification because you actually measure its performance.
6. Ed25519 signatures and identity authentication
Major — security architecture
Your use of per-fragment Ed25519 signatures is interesting.
The receiver can authenticate the sender independently of the shared symmetric session keys.
This is valuable in a multi-node environment, particularly where multiple participants share session material.
However, there is a subtle distinction:
A valid Ed25519 signature proves possession of the corresponding private key. It does not independently prove that the key belongs to the intended device.
That association must be established during pairing.
Your current four-character pairing protocol has weaknesses in precisely this area.
Consequently, the identity guarantee of your per-fragment signatures inherits the limitations of your pairing procedure.
I would therefore avoid describing the Full profile as providing robust authenticated peer identity until the pairing mechanism is strengthened.

I would not simply remove per-fragment signatures, though. Your proposed alternatives must preserve the sender-authentication guarantee that motivated them.
7. Replay protection and the sliding bitmap
Good design, with an important performance weakness
Your replay-window condition is:
\[ s>s_{max}\quad \lor \]
\[ (s_{max}-s<64)\land W[s_{max}-s]=0 \]
This is a conventional sliding-window approach.
The important design decision is that the bitmap is committed only after both the Ascon tag and Ed25519 signature have passed verification.
I agree with this.
Otherwise, an attacker could potentially send unauthenticated frames with artificially high sequence numbers and advance the replay window, excluding legitimate packets.
Your retransmission problem
Your paper identifies an interesting timing interaction:
ARQ timeout: 600 ms.
Ed25519 verification: approximately 663 ms.
Replay-window commit: after verification.
Consider the following:
Retransmission race
0 ms — Original frame arrives
Replay check passes because its sequence number is new.
600 ms — Retransmission arrives
The original verification has not completed. The replay bitmap has not yet committed the sequence number.
600–1,263 ms — Duplicate verification
Both frames can consume expensive Ed25519 verification work.
After verification
Duplicate suppression eventually prevents the duplicate from being accepted as a new message.
This is a genuine architectural problem.
Your proposed four-slot verification cache is a good idea, but I would not implement it without a few safeguards.
The cache should:
Be associated with the current session.
Include the complete frame digest.
Never accept a different payload merely because its sequence number matches.
Be invalidated after re-keying.
Be integrated with the replay-window state.
Have a clearly defined concurrency and cache-eviction policy.
Your suggested 16-byte digest is reasonable as an engineering compromise, but it is not mathematically collision-free. The full cryptographic analysis should account for that residual collision probability.
My verdict: Keep the replay-window architecture. The major improvement should be the handling of in-flight verification, not changing the basic sliding-window principle.

9. Reliability and error-control mathematics
Your Hamming(15,11) implementation is a reasonable choice for a compact single-error-correcting code.
The nominal code rate is:

The parity overhead relative to the original data is:
\[ \frac{4}{11}\times100=36.36\% \]
Your stated value of approximately 36.4% is correct.
The important caveat
Hamming(15,11) is a single-error-correcting code, not a double-error-correcting code.
A double-bit error may be miscorrected.
Your downstream CRC-32 and Ascon authentication provide additional detection mechanisms, which is sensible.
However, the CRC should be treated as an error-detection mechanism, not a cryptographic authenticator.
The actual cryptographic integrity guarantee comes from Ascon.

10. Channel hopping: mathematical and implementation discrepancy
Major — implementation not validated
Your intended channel-hopping algorithm uses a nine-channel permutation:
\[ p\in\{1,6,11,3,8,2,7,9,4\} \]
with a nominal five-second update interval.
However, you disclose the following implementation:
\[ n=e\land\neg1 \]
\[ k=(n\gg6)\bmod256 \]
\[ index=(n\gg14)\bmod9 \]
You report that this produces a 320-second dwell time instead of five seconds.
This is an important finding.
The implementation has a mismatch between the intended epoch calculation and the actual bit shifts.
I would recommend replacing this with a much simpler explicit epoch calculation:
\[ epoch=\left\lfloor\frac{t}{5000}\right\rfloor \]
Then derive the permutation and channel index from that epoch.
More importantly, I would not use the current hopping algorithm in any security claim.
Your paper correctly says that hopping was disabled during all evaluated campaigns.
One additional point: channel hopping is not inherently a cryptographic security mechanism. It can improve interference avoidance and complicate passive observation, but it should not be presented as a substitute for encryption, authentication, or replay protection.
11. Concurrency, memory management, and denial of service
This is an area where I think your paper has a particularly interesting engineering contribution.
You use:
A 48 KB static bump allocator.
A 32-entry session RX queue.
Separate FreeRTOS tasks across both cores.
A cost-ordered receive pipeline.
No application-level heap allocation in the session/crypto path.
These are sensible design choices.
However, your own measurements expose a potential denial-of-service vulnerability.
The receive queue problem
You report:
Approximately 663 ms Ed25519 verification.
A 32-entry RX queue.
Sustained incoming traffic.
Queue overflow after approximately 71 fragments in the described back-to-back scenario.
This creates an asymmetric workload problem.
An attacker does not necessarily need to break the cryptography.
They may simply exploit the fact that legitimate traffic arrives faster than your receiver can authenticate it.
Your outer HMAC filter helps, but it does not eliminate the problem.
The manuscript estimates that approximately 100 forged frames per second can saturate Core 1.
I would consider this one of the most important reliability/security limitations in the current Full profile.
What I would implement
I would investigate:
A bounded per-peer verification queue.
Duplicate-frame suppression before public-key verification.
Rate limiting for unauthenticated traffic.
A maximum verification-work budget per time interval.
Prioritization of authenticated control traffic.
A mechanism to prevent one peer from monopolizing the verification pipeline.
Be careful with rate limiting, though: an unauthenticated attacker must not be able to permanently block legitimate traffic simply by occupying the queue.
Static memory claims
Your claim of zero application-level heap allocation is appropriately qualified.
You explicitly exclude allocations performed internally by BLE, Wi-Fi, and mbedTLS.
Keep that qualification.

12. Three additional issues I would investigate
These are not necessarily confirmed vulnerabilities. They are questions that cannot be settled from the manuscript alone.
A. Signature and header binding
Your receive pipeline authenticates the ESL header through Ascon associated data and verifies the Ed25519 signature over the outer ciphertext.
I would like to establish exactly how the following are connected:
\[ Header\rightarrow AD\rightarrow Ascon\ Tag \]
\[ Ciphertext\rightarrow Ed25519\ Signature \]
The construction can be valid, but the specification should explain why the signed ciphertext and authenticated header together bind the complete logical frame.
In particular, document the treatment of the length prefix, nonce, HMAC tag, and protocol context.
B. Sequence-number and nonce-counter consistency
Your manuscript uses both the terms esl_seq and nonce_counter.
I would like to know whether these are:
The same 64-bit counter.
Separate counters with synchronized advancement.
A sequence number and an independently generated nonce counter.
This matters because the replay window, nonce uniqueness, duplicate cache, and retransmission logic all depend on their exact relationship.
I recommend defining a single canonical sequence of events for every transmitted fragment.
C. The MAC acknowledgement interpretation
Your paper describes a 14-byte 802.11 MAC ACK and gives an associated frame-control value of 0x1D.
I would verify that value against your actual PCAP captures and the IEEE 802.11 frame format before publication.
A standard ACK control frame is normally identified by frame-control value 0x00D4 (in little-endian byte order, D4 00).
If 0x1D refers to a different field or a custom driver event, it should not be labelled as the standard ACK frame-control value.
This is a small detail, but it is exactly the sort of thing a networking reviewer might catch.
